The short version

  • RAVE is an autoencoder. An encoder squeezes audio into a short list of numbers about 21 times a second; a decoder turns those numbers back into audio that sounds like whatever it was trained on.
  • The sliders are the decoder's input. In rave-1-sliders you replace the encoder with your hand. Timbre transfer is the same decoder fed by the encoder instead.
  • Each model has a fixed number of knobs (4, 8 or 16 here), picked when it was exported. Training always produces 128; the export keeps the few that vary most and sorts them, so the first is the biggest pattern in the training data.
  • “Biggest pattern” is statistical, not designed. On the percussion model the first knob turned out to be loud versus silent.
  • BRAVE is RAVE rebuilt for live playing: ~10 ms latency instead of 250–440 ms, 344 latent frames a second instead of 21.5, a quarter of the weights. Same training tools.
  • But no pretrained BRAVE models are downloadable, and its export drops the sorting step, so its first knobs are not the important ones until we put that back.

How RAVE works

Caillon and Esling, IRCAM, 2021. The model has three parts:

Training happens in two stages from a source

  1. Learn the representation. Encoder and decoder train together to rebuild the input. The loss compares spectra, not waveforms, so the model is not punished for getting phase wrong, and phase detail stays out of the latent space. A second term (the VAE's KL term) pulls every latent number toward a bell curve centred on 0. That is why 0 is “typical” and ±3 is the edge.
  2. Make it sound good. The encoder is frozen. Only the decoder keeps training, now against a discriminator that tries to tell real audio from decoded audio. Freezing matters: letting the encoder train here inflates the number of dimensions it uses and makes the space harder to explore.

The decoder also has two helpers that come up later: a loudness branch that scales the output (it is why silence stays clean) and a filtered noise generator for breathy and noisy sounds. To reach 48 kHz cheaply, the waveform is split into 16 frequency bands (PQMF) and generated band by band.

The knobs: why 4, 8 or 16

After training, the export runs the encoder over the training audio and does a PCA on the 128 numbers: it rotates them so dimension 1 is the direction where the training audio differs most, dimension 2 the next, and so on. Then it keeps the top few, enough to reach a chosen share of the variation (the “fidelity”). The rest are held neutral when decoding. Each .ts file records these shares, so we can draw them:

Share of the training audio's latent variation carried by each kept dimension, read from each RAVE v2 file's fidelity buffer measured. Hover a bar for the number. The v1 files (percussion, VCTK, nasa, vintage) do not record this.

Two things to see. The ordering is real but its strength varies: sol_ordinario's first knob carries a third of everything, while musicnet's first sixteen are nearly level. The cut is a choice someone made: sol_ordinario stops at 92%, and the fifth dimension it dropped carries another 7.8%. With a training checkpoint you can re-export the same model with more or fewer knobs. IRCAM did not publish checkpoints for these, so that only works on models we train.

What we measured

Percussion's first knob is a loudness gate measured

Dragging z1 below zero on the percussion model goes nearly silent whatever the other three do. We decoded it outside Max with 30 random settings of z2–z4 at each z1 value:

reading In eight hours of percussion, the biggest difference between moments is a hit versus the silence between hits, so the strongest direction became “how much energy is here”. The loudness branch learned to go quiet wherever training audio was quiet. Nobody designed knob 1 to be volume. It matters for phone-rave: on this model, tilting the phone one way mutes it.

The knobs all have about the same range measured

We encoded 30 s of tudor.wav and looked at how far each dimension moved. Spread was similar across dimensions: about 0.5–0.8 on sol_ordinario, percussion and VCTK, about 1 on musicnet. So a higher rank does not mean a wider range, and ±3 is a fair slider range for every bar. Musicnet is the exception: real audio reached −7.4 to +6.8, past what the sliders can reach. Tudor is not what any of these models trained on, so treat the ranges as approximate.

RAVE in Max

  • nn~ <model> decode has one signal inlet per knob and outputs audio. encode goes the other way; forward does both (timbre transfer).
  • Models are found by name on Max's search path (Max 8/Library/rave-models, a symlink to the IRCAM folder).
  • nn~ cannot change model while running, so the patches delete the box and script a new one when you choose a model.
  • We run nn~ 1.5.6: 1.6.0 calls a Max 9 API and will not load in Max 8.
  • nn~ processes audio in buffers, which adds latency on top of the model's own. This matters more for BRAVE than for RAVE.

The catches for us

  • Will it load in nn~? Yes, according to the README. BRAVE uses RAVE's own rave export, which writes a normal .ts. But the README warns these “might show higher latency than the BRAVE Plugin due to a different audio buffering strategy”. The 10 ms figure is from their engine, not nn~. We have to measure it in nn~ with a small buffer.
  • Are there pretrained models? Effectively no. The paper's models (sax from Filosax, drums from E-GMD) are said to be on the project page, but there is no download link, and the plugin uses its own .h5 format.
  • The first knobs are not the important ones. The scripted models skip the latent projection, which is the PCA sorting described above. The paper calls latent manipulation outside its scope. For slider and phone control we must keep or recompute that projection at export, or z1–z4 are four arbitrary dimensions of 128.
  • It was only evaluated for timbre transfer, never driven by hand or by sensors. That part would be ours.

Why the phone demo cares

Sarah Nabi's I1 “direct motion exploration” (MOCO '24) maps body motion straight onto the first latent dimensions. Our phone-rave simplifies it: attitude pitch and roll → z1 and z2, spin → z3, shake → z4. Two RAVE properties limit how direct that feels:

  • 21.5 frames a second. The phone sends ~60 Hz; RAVE hears a new value every 46 ms. BRAVE hears one every 2.9 ms. Note that sol_ordinario_fast (512 : 1, ~86 frames a second) already lets us hear part of this difference without training anything.
  • Latency. Hundreds of milliseconds between gesture and sound breaks the feeling of playing. BRAVE was designed to fix exactly this.

And one property we found: on models like percussion, z1 is a loudness gate, so whatever motion drives z1 decides whether there is sound at all.

Training both on the same material

BRAVE trains with RAVE's own command-line tools and reads RAVE's preprocessed datasets from a source. So one preprocessed dataset can train a RAVE v2 and a BRAVE, and the only difference you hear is the architecture.

# once, on a compute node (never the SLURM login node)
pip install h5py acids-rave==2.3          # the version BRAVE pins
rave preprocess --input_path drums/ --output_path drums_pre/

# two models, same data
rave train --config v2    --name drums_rave  --db_path drums_pre/
rave train --config ./configs/brave.gin --name drums_brave --db_path drums_pre/

# export each twice, to hear what the knob cut costs
rave export --run runs/drums_rave  ...     # 4-dim and 16-dim versions
rave export --run runs/drums_brave ...     # keep the latent projection on

The paper's schedule is 1M representation steps plus 500k adversarial steps. RAVE v1 took ~6 days on a TITAN V; on the cluster's L40S GPUs expect a few days per model reading, not yet measured. Export flags for the dimension count and the projection still need checking against the 2.3 code.

In class

  1. Preprocess a small folder of sound.
  2. Train for a few thousand steps. It will sound bad, and the early sound shows the representation stage before the adversarial stage starts.
  3. Export, drop the .ts into rave-models, play it in rave-1-sliders.
  4. Switch to the full-length models trained in the background and compare.

The patch progression

  1. rave-1-sliders (built): the decoder by hand. One bar per knob, model menu, link to each model's data.
  2. rave-2-encode: a sound file into the encoder and out the decoder (timbre transfer), its latents shown slowly beside the bars.
  3. rave-3-phone: the existing phone-rave, motion in place of the sliders.
  4. rave-4-compare: one material, RAVE versus BRAVE, on one switch. Needs the training runs.
